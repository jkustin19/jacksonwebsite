For a long time, I thought I was going into commercial real estate. I studied real estate and finance at Florida State, graduated, and went into brokerage. I liked the business. I still do. But I kept having meetings with clients and thinking the same thing:

I want to be on that side of the table.

I didn't want to spend my career helping someone else build what they owned. I wanted to figure out how to build things of my own.

Around the same time, I started getting serious about AI. And I got hooked. Not because I thought AI was cool, but because I could see how much work people were still doing manually that they simply didn't need to do anymore.

Research. Admin. Follow-up. Repetitive processes. Moving information from one place to another. Hours of work that technology could now handle in minutes.

The technology was already here. The hard part was knowing what to do with it, and actually building it.

So I started learning everything I could. I tested models. Tried tools. Built workflows. Made apps. Automated pieces of my own life. Broke things. Rebuilt them. And kept going.

I can sit down to build an AI system at night, look up, and realize hours have passed. There's always another thing I want to try.

The scratch golfer analogy

I'm around scratch.

I've played golf almost my entire life. I was recruited to play in college before COVID changed those plans, and I still compete and play every chance I get. Today, I'm a little better than scratch.

Someone once asked me how good I am at AI. The best comparison I could come up with was golf:

I know what I'm doing. I've put in the reps. I can perform at a high level. And I'm nowhere near done getting better.

That's probably why AI fits me so well. The target keeps moving. There's always something new to learn, test, build, or improve. I love that.

Why I build

“Wait… I don't have to do this anymore?”

My favorite part isn't the technology. It's showing someone something I built for them and watching that moment land. That reaction never gets old. Because getting rid of repetitive work isn't really about automation. It's about getting your time back.

Time to sell. Time to think. Time to create. Time to grow the business. Time to do the parts of your job that you're actually good at. That's what I want to build.
